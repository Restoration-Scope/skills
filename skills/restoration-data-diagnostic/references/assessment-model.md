# Assessment model

Never reduce the diagnosis to one score. Rate each domain (intervention areas, people and organizations, interventions, monitoring, supporting evidence) on two separate dimensions, and show the evidence for each rating.

## Traceability stage

| Stage | Name | Means |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Absent | The source or record set cannot be found. |
| 1 | Located but fragmented | Relevant data exists but is split, inconsistently named or hard to assemble. |
| 2 | Organized | The sources and their intended scope can be identified. |
| 3 | Structured and identifiable | Records follow a repeatable structure and use stable identifiers. |
| 4 | Connected and traceable | Places, people, activities, evidence and later observations can be related to each other. |
| 5 | Current and decision-ready | The team can get the answer often enough, and with enough context, to act on it. |

A domain sits at the highest stage it fully meets. When it sits between two, pick the lower and say why. Name what is missing to reach the next one.

## Quality dimensions

Rate each with the evidence, using:

- **Good:** holds for all or nearly all of the records checked; the exceptions are named.
- **Partial:** holds for most records, with gaps that are countable and fixable.
- **Poor:** fails for a large share of records, or for the records a key question depends on.
- **Unknown:** the evidence cannot support a rating (no denominator, sample only, nothing to compare against).
- **Not applicable:** the dimension does not apply to this domain or scope.

Prefer unknown to a guess.

- **Exhaustive:** every in-scope unit and every expected activity is represented.
- **Complete:** each represented record holds the information and evidence it should.
- **Correct:** the information is attached to the right real-world thing and describes it accurately.
- **Current:** recent enough for the decision or obligation agreed in scope.
- **Sourced:** origin, owner and confidence of the information are known.

Keep them apart. All 100 farms may be present (exhaustive) while some lack coordinates (incomplete), and a coordinate may point to the wrong place (incorrect). Five hundred rows can still miss required records; ten rows can be exhaustive for a ten-site project.

## Testing exhaustiveness

Row counts are not the test. Exhaustiveness needs a denominator: the full set the data was meant to cover.

1. **Define the universe.** Which set is being tested (all actual intervention sites, all farmers enrolled in a season, all planting events in a campaign, all monitoring rounds required per site, all documents required per contract) and for which period. "All farms" is ambiguous until you know whether it means enrolled, active, planted or monitored.
2. **Find the expected obligation.** For repeated activities, what should exist for each member: a fixed rule (five monitoring visits per site) or a conditional one (by season, intervention type, risk). Find it in plans, protocols, contracts or schedules, or ask the team. Never invent it. If no one knows it, that is a foundational gap.
3. **Match records to the universe** by stable identifiers wherever possible. Records that match no member never count toward coverage; list them as unmatched.
4. **Compare expected and observed** per unit, not only in total:

   | Unit | Expected | Observed | Gap | Notes |
   |---|---:|---:|---:|---|
   | Site 001 | 5 monitoring visits | 5 | 0 | Covered |
   | Site 002 | 5 monitoring visits | 2 | 3 | Follow-up needed |
   | Site 003 | 5 monitoring visits | 7 | 0 | Two extra; check for duplicates |

   Show missing members, missing expected records, duplicates, unmatched records and legitimate exceptions. A good total can hide uneven coverage.
5. **Record source and confidence** for every expected total or rule: where it came from, who owns it, when it was last updated, whether it is authoritative or inferred.

Refuse to give a coverage percentage when the denominator is unknown, comes only from the data being tested, or conflicts between sources. Say which of these applies.
